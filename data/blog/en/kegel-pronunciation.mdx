The kegel pronunciation most people reach for is wrong. It is **KAY-gul** — long A like cake, hard G like goat, stress on the first syllable, and a second syllable that rhymes with dull rather than bell.

The usual mistakes are KEE-gul, borrowed from words like legal and regal, and kuh-GEL, which puts the stress in the wrong place and turns the ending into gel. Neither is correct, and the reason why is a small piece of medical history.

## Say It Out Loud

Break it into two beats:

1. **KAY** — as in the letter K, or the first syllable of cable. Not kee, not keh.
2. **gul** — a soft, unstressed ending. Like the end of eagle or bagel, not like the word gel.

Put together: **KAY-gul**. The stress lands hard on the first syllable and the second almost disappears, the same shape as bagel, nagle or Hegel.

If you want a memory hook, bagel and kegel rhyme. That is the whole trick, and it is the fastest way to stop second-guessing yourself in front of a doctor.

## Where the Name Comes From

Kegel is a surname, which is exactly why English spelling rules lead you astray. Words with a -egel ending that came through Latin or French — legal, regal — take the long E. A German family name does not.

The exercises are named after **Arnold Henry Kegel (1894–1972)**, an American gynaecologist who taught at what is now the Keck School of Medicine of USC. In 1948 he published a non-surgical approach to pelvic floor weakness, "The nonsurgical treatment of genital relaxation," in *Annals of Western Medicine and Surgery*. He also invented the **perineometer**, a pressure device inserted into the vagina to measure the strength of a voluntary pelvic floor contraction.

The perineometer is the part that gets overlooked, and it is the part that made his work stick. Before it, pelvic floor training was guesswork — no way to confirm a patient was contracting the right muscle, and no way to measure progress. Kegel gave clinicians a number. You can read more on his background at [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnold_Kegel).

## What Clinicians Actually Call Them

Once you can say it, you will notice that specialists often do not. In journals and clinical guidelines the standard term is **pelvic floor muscle training**, usually abbreviated to PFMT. In the UK the everyday phrasing is pelvic floor exercises. In the US, kegels.

All three mean the same thing: voluntarily contracting and releasing the sling of muscle that runs from your pubic bone to your tailbone. [NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/urologic-diseases/kegel-exercises) and [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/articles/kegel-exercises) both use the kegel label alongside the clinical one.

Knowing the synonyms is useful beyond trivia. Searching for "pelvic floor muscle training" pulls up the actual evidence base, while searching for "kegels" mostly pulls up product listings.

## What You Are Actually Contracting

The pelvic floor is a hammock of skeletal muscle slung between the pubic bone at the front and the coccyx at the back, wrapping around the urethra and anus — and the vagina in women. It has two jobs that matter here: holding those openings closed at rest, and clamping down fast when abdominal pressure spikes.

In men, the same muscle group also compresses the veins at the base of the penis during an erection, which is what keeps blood trapped and the erection firm. Our explainer on [pelvic floor muscles](/blog/pelvic-floor-muscles) walks through the anatomy properly, and [kegels for men](/blog/kegels-for-men) covers the male-specific side.

To find the muscle, imagine stopping the flow of urine midstream and simultaneously holding back wind. You should feel an inward-and-upward lift with no movement in your stomach, thighs or buttocks. That lift is the contraction. (Use the urine-stopping image to locate it — do not make a habit of actually stopping your stream, which can interfere with normal emptying.)

## How to Train It

The basic protocol is not complicated, and [Harvard Health](https://www.health.harvard.edu/bladder-and-bowel/step-by-step-guide-to-performing-kegel-exercises) lays out a similar version:

1. **Slow holds.** Lift, hold for 5 seconds, then release completely for 5 seconds. Ten repetitions.
2. **Quick lifts.** Ten rapid contractions with no hold. These train the fast reflex that stops a leak when you cough.
3. **Breathe throughout.** Exhale gently as you lift. Holding your breath recruits the wrong muscles and pushes pressure downward, which is the opposite of what you want.
4. **Three sessions a day.** Under three minutes total.
5. **Progress gradually.** Add one second to your holds each week until you reach a clean 10 seconds without your abs bracing.

The release matters as much as the squeeze. A muscle that never fully lets go builds tension rather than strength, and that is how people end up with an overactive pelvic floor instead of a stronger one.

## Common Mistakes

- **Squeezing the glutes or thighs.** If your seat lifts off the chair, you are using the wrong muscles. Put a hand on your abdomen to check nothing bulges.
- **Holding your breath.** Almost universal at the start. It spikes intra-abdominal pressure and works against you.
- **Skipping the release.** Half the exercise. Rushing it trains tension, not strength.
- **Doing them only when symptoms show up.** Pelvic floor muscle is skeletal muscle. It needs weeks of repeated stimulus, not a burst of effort when you notice a leak.
- **Assuming more is better.** If urinating starts slowly, if you have pelvic pain, or if you feel permanently clenched, your floor may be overactive rather than weak. [How to tell if your pelvic floor is tight or weak](/blog/how-to-tell-if-pelvic-floor-is-tight-or-weak) explains how to check before you add volume.

## What to Expect

Most people who train consistently notice better bladder control somewhere between weeks four and six, with fuller strength gains around three months. That timeline holds whether you call it a kegel, a pelvic floor exercise, or PFMT. If nothing has changed after three months of genuine consistency, the issue is usually technique — a pelvic health physiotherapist can confirm in one appointment whether you are contracting correctly.

So: **KAY-gul**, rhymes with bagel, named after a gynaecologist who gave the field its first way to measure the thing. Now the pronunciation is out of the way, the only part left is doing them.
