Most men only look up penis diseases after spotting something that worries them. The good news is that **the vast majority of penile problems are common, well understood and treatable** — and a handful of warning signs tell you which ones need a doctor today versus a conversation next week.

This guide covers the conditions men actually run into, what each one does, and where training the muscles around the penis fits in.

## The Main Categories of Penis Diseases

Penile conditions fall into five broad groups. Knowing the group tells you a lot about what to do next.

- **Functional problems** — erectile dysfunction and priapism, where the erection mechanism itself misfires
- **Structural problems** — Peyronie’s disease, where scar tissue changes the shape of the penis
- **Inflammatory and skin conditions** — balanitis, lichen sclerosus and similar problems affecting the head and foreskin
- **Foreskin problems** — phimosis and paraphimosis
- **Infections and cancer** — sexually transmitted infections and, rarely, penile cancer

## Erectile Dysfunction

ED is by far the most common penis disease. The [Massachusetts Male Aging Study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8254833/) found that **around 52% of men aged 40 to 70 had some degree of erectile dysfunction**, ranging from mild to complete.

An erection is a hydraulic event. Arteries open, the spongy chambers fill with blood, and the veins that drain them get compressed. Anything that damages blood vessels — high blood pressure, diabetes, smoking, excess weight — tends to show up in the penis early, because its arteries are narrow.

The other half of the mechanism is muscular. The ischiocavernosus and bulbospongiosus muscles wrap the base of the penis and squeeze to raise pressure inside it. When they are weak, erections soften faster even when blood flow is fine.

That is why ED is often worth treating as a warning light for heart health. Our guide to [erectile dysfunction treatment](/blog/erectile-dysfunction-treatment) covers the full range of options.

## Peyronie’s Disease

Peyronie’s disease is a build-up of fibrous scar tissue, called plaque, inside the penis. It causes a curve, an indentation or shortening, and often pain during erections in the early phase.

It is more common than most men assume. A [population-based study of 11,420 US men](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22110491/) estimated prevalence at **0.5% for confirmed diagnoses and up to 13% when symptoms and treatment history were included**.

Not every curve is Peyronie’s. Many men have a natural bend they have had since puberty, which is harmless. The difference is change: a new curve, a lump you can feel, or pain that was not there before. Our dedicated article on [Peyronie’s disease](/blog/peyronie-disease) explains the two phases and the treatments, and [curved penis](/blog/curved-penis) covers when a bend is simply normal.

## Priapism

Priapism is an erection that will not go down, lasting four hours or more and usually unrelated to arousal. **It is a medical emergency.**

The most common form, ischaemic priapism, traps blood in the penis. That blood loses oxygen, and after a few hours the tissue starts to be damaged. The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/symptoms/priapism-painful-erections/) advises hospital treatment as soon as possible for an erection lasting more than 3 to 4 hours, to avoid permanent damage.

Causes include ED injections, some antidepressants and antipsychotics, sickle cell disease, and recreational drugs. If an erection has lasted four hours, go to an emergency department. Do not wait it out.

## Balanitis and Other Inflammatory Conditions

Balanitis is inflammation of the head of the penis. It causes redness, soreness, itching, and sometimes a discharge or unpleasant smell under the foreskin.

It is common, particularly in uncircumcised men. [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK537143/) estimates it affects **3 to 11% of men at some point in their lives**, and it is far more common in men with diabetes, partly because sugar in urine feeds yeast. Thrush, bacterial infection, irritation from soaps and poor hygiene are the usual triggers.

Most cases clear with gentle washing in plain water, keeping the area dry, and an antifungal or antibiotic cream if needed. The [NHS guide to balanitis](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/balanitis/) recommends seeing a GP if it does not settle.

Lichen sclerosus is less common but worth knowing. It causes white, tight, shiny patches on the head or foreskin and can gradually narrow the foreskin opening. It needs medical treatment, usually a strong steroid cream, and follow-up.

Harmless spots are far more common than disease, though. Pearly papules and Fordyce spots are normal anatomy. See [penile papules on the shaft](/blog/penile-papules-on-shaft) if that is what you are looking at.

## Phimosis and Paraphimosis

**Phimosis** is a foreskin too tight to pull back over the head of the penis. It is normal in young boys, but in adult men it can cause pain during erections, trouble cleaning and repeated infections. Steroid creams and gentle stretching often help; circumcision is the definitive fix.

**Paraphimosis** is the reverse: the foreskin is pulled back and gets stuck, swelling behind the head of the penis. Swelling cuts off blood flow, so this is an emergency. The [NHS page on tight foreskin](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/phimosis/) advises going to A&E if the foreskin cannot be returned.

## Infections and Penile Cancer

Sexually transmitted infections — herpes, genital warts, syphilis, chlamydia and gonorrhoea — can all show up as sores, blisters, warts or discharge. Testing is quick, most are curable and the rest are very manageable, and many sexual health clinics see you without a referral.

Penile cancer is rare. The [American Cancer Society](https://www.cancer.org/cancer/types/penile-cancer/key-statistics.html) reports it is diagnosed in **fewer than 1 in 100,000 men** in the US each year, mostly in men over 60. HPV infection, smoking and untreated phimosis raise the risk. The key sign is a sore, lump or patch of changed skin that does not heal over several weeks.

## Warning Signs Worth Acting On

Go today, to an emergency department:

1. An erection lasting four hours or more
2. A foreskin stuck behind the head of the penis
3. A sudden cracking sound during sex followed by pain, swelling and bruising — this can be a penile fracture

Book a doctor within a week or two:

1. A new curve, lump or pain during erections
2. A sore, blister or rash that has not cleared in two to three weeks
3. Discharge, burning when you pee, or any possible STI exposure
4. Erections that have steadily softened over months

## Where Pelvic Floor Training Fits In

Kegels will not clear an infection or dissolve Peyronie’s plaque. What they do is strengthen the muscles that control erection rigidity, ejaculation and bladder function.

The evidence is strongest for ED. In a [randomised trial by Dorey and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15527607/) in the British Journal of General Practice, pelvic floor exercises with biofeedback restored normal erectile function in **40% of men** with ED and improved it in a further 34.5%. That makes training a sensible partner to medical care for the most common penis disease of all.

The basic routine:

1. Squeeze as if stopping your urine stream and lifting your testicles, keeping your belly and buttocks relaxed.
2. Hold for 5 seconds, then release fully for 5 seconds. Repeat 10 times.
3. Add 10 quick one-second squeezes.
4. Do this three times a day. Build toward 10-second holds over several weeks.

## What Progress Looks Like

For ED, men who train consistently usually notice firmer erections somewhere around weeks 6 to 12. The Dorey trial assessed results at three months and six months.

Infections such as balanitis typically improve within one to two weeks of treatment. Peyronie’s disease follows a slower course: the painful phase usually settles within 12 to 18 months, after which treatment focuses on the curve.

## The Bottom Line

Most penis diseases are common and treatable. **Erectile dysfunction leads the list, followed by inflammatory skin problems, Peyronie’s disease and foreskin conditions**, with penile cancer genuinely rare.

Know the emergencies, get anything persistent checked, and train the muscles you can control. The last part is the one that is entirely in your hands.
