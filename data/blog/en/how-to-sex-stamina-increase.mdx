Most advice on sex stamina is either a supplement ad or a distraction technique. Think about baseball, breathe deeply, use a thicker condom. None of that builds anything — it just tries to dampen arousal in the moment, which is why it stops working the second you are genuinely into it.

Stamina is a physical capacity, and physical capacities respond to training. Three systems set your ceiling: the pelvic floor muscles that control the ejaculatory reflex, the cardiovascular system that supplies and sustains an erection, and the arousal awareness that lets you spot the point of no return before you cross it. All three are trainable, all three have research behind them, and none of them require a prescription.

Here is how to increase sex stamina in a way that holds up after the novelty wears off.

## Why Training Works When Tricks Do Not

Ejaculation is a reflex, and reflexes are driven by muscles. The bulbospongiosus and the surrounding pelvic floor contract rhythmically to expel semen, which means those muscles are not bystanders in the timing — they are the mechanism. Strengthen them and you gain real influence over something that previously felt automatic.

The clinical evidence is stronger than most men expect. In a study by [Pastore and colleagues published in Therapeutic Advances in Urology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4003840/), 40 men with lifelong premature ejaculation and a baseline latency under a minute went through 12 weeks of pelvic floor rehabilitation. Thirty-three of them — **82.5%** — regained control of the ejaculatory reflex. Mean latency rose from about 40 seconds to 146.2 seconds, and the men followed up at six months still averaged 112.6 seconds. The gains outlasted the program.

The erection half of stamina has its own evidence base. A [systematic review and meta-analysis of 11 randomised controlled trials in The Journal of Sexual Medicine](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37814532/) found aerobic exercise improved erectile function scores by a mean of 2.8 points, with bigger gains for men who started worse: 2.3 points for mild dysfunction, 3.3 for moderate, and 4.9 for severe. And the landmark pelvic floor trial by Dorey and colleagues in the [British Journal of General Practice](https://bjgp.org/content/54/508/819) showed three months of twice-daily pelvic floor exercise beat lifestyle advice alone for erectile function.

Two different muscle systems, two different outcomes, same principle: train the tissue, not the mood.

## The Step-by-Step Routine

### 1. Find the right muscle

Stop your urine mid-stream once — that is the contraction. You can also stand in front of a mirror and lift your testicles slightly without moving anything else. Use the stop-stream test to identify the muscle, then never again as an exercise, since habitually interrupting urination can irritate the bladder.

If your abs, glutes, or thighs tighten, you are recruiting the wrong things. The contraction should feel like a lift and squeeze deep inside, with everything else still.

### 2. Train both contraction types

This is the part most men get wrong — they do one style forever and plateau.

1. **Quick pulses.** One-second contractions, full release between each. Ten to fifteen reps. These build the fast-twitch response that matters at the moment you need to hold back.
2. **Long holds.** Contract for five seconds, release for five. Build up to ten-second holds. Ten reps. These build the endurance that keeps blood trapped through a longer session.
3. **Full release matters as much as the squeeze.** A muscle that never fully relaxes is not getting stronger, it is getting tight — and a tight pelvic floor causes its own problems.

Do both sets twice a day. That is the frequency the erection trials used.

### 3. Add the cardio layer

Erections are a plumbing problem before they are anything else. Aim for roughly 40 minutes of moderate aerobic work four times a week — the volume most of the trials in that meta-analysis used. Brisk walking on an incline, cycling, swimming, or rowing all qualify. The [NHS guidance on erection problems](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/erection-problems-erectile-dysfunction/) names regular exercise and weight management as first-line changes for exactly this reason.

Blood-flow adaptations take about eight weeks to register. Our guide on [how to increase blood flow to the penis naturally](/blog/how-to-increase-blood-flow-to-penis-naturally) covers what else moves that needle.

### 4. Train arousal awareness

Strength gives you the brake. Awareness tells you when to use it. Practise on your own with the stop-start method: build arousal to roughly 7 out of 10, stop completely, let it drop to a 4, then resume. Three or four cycles per session.

The goal is not endurance for its own sake — it is learning where your point of no return sits so it stops arriving as a surprise. Our walkthroughs of the [stop-start method](/blog/stop-start-method-for-premature-ejaculation) and the [squeeze technique](/blog/squeeze-method-premature-ejaculation) cover the mechanics in detail.

### 5. Fix the obvious drains

Sleep under six hours, heavy drinking before sex, and untreated high blood pressure all cut into stamina through the same vascular and hormonal channels. These are not marginal. Alcohol in particular is a depressant acting directly on the arousal response — worth reading [how alcohol affects a man sexually](/blog/how-does-alcohol-affect-a-man-sexually) if it is part of your routine.

## Building the Habit

Every study cited above ran a structured protocol for twelve weeks. That is the actual variable, and it is the one men lose track of first.

The failure pattern is predictable. Weeks one and two feel like nothing is happening, because nothing is — you are learning the movement, not building strength yet. Week three, the reminder gets ignored. Week four, the routine quietly ends, and the conclusion becomes "kegels do not work for me."

Two things prevent that. Anchor the sets to something you already do every day — the drive to work, brushing your teeth, the first ten minutes at your desk. And track the reps, because a number you can look at is much harder to lie to yourself about than a vague sense that you have been "pretty consistent."

## Common Mistakes

**Squeezing everything.** If your stomach pulls in or your thighs tense, the pelvic floor is doing a fraction of the work. Put a hand on your abdomen and check.

**Only doing long holds.** Endurance holds are the popular version, but the quick pulses are what fire in the moment you need control. Skipping them trains half the system.

**Never relaxing.** Some men already have an overactive pelvic floor rather than a weak one — the signs are pelvic pain, urinary urgency, or a persistently semi-firm penis at rest. Adding more contractions makes that worse. Our guide on [telling if your pelvic floor is tight or weak](/blog/how-to-tell-if-pelvic-floor-is-tight-or-weak) covers the distinction, and it is worth two minutes before you start.

**Training only in bed.** Lying down is the easiest position because gravity is not fighting you. Progress to sitting, then standing, or the strength never transfers to the situation you care about.

**Judging it at week three.** The trials measured at twelve weeks. Judging your results at three is like weighing yourself after one gym session.

## Common Questions

**Will this help if my problem is staying hard, not finishing too fast?** Yes — the same muscles do both jobs. The ischiocavernosus and bulbospongiosus compress the veins that drain the penis, which is what maintains firmness once blood is in. See [how to stay hard](/blog/how-to-stay-hard) for the full mechanism.

**Do supplements help?** Nothing over the counter has evidence close to what exercise has. If you have a genuine deficiency, correcting it matters; otherwise the money is better spent elsewhere.

**Can I do too many kegels?** Yes. More than two structured sessions a day tends to produce soreness and tightness rather than faster progress. Muscles adapt during recovery, not during work.

**Does this work after 50?** The mechanism is the same at any age, and the meta-analysis found the largest gains in men who started with the worst baseline scores. If anything, there is more room to improve.

## The Bottom Line

Increasing sex stamina comes down to three trainable systems and twelve weeks of doing the work: pelvic floor strength for the reflex, aerobic fitness for the blood flow, and arousal awareness for the timing. The research is clear that all three respond — 82.5% of men in Pastore's study regained ejaculatory control, and aerobic training improved erectile function across 11 randomised trials.
