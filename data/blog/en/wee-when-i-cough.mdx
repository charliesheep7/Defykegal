If you wee a little when you cough, your bladder is not the problem. Your timing is.

A cough is a pressure event. It fires in roughly a tenth of a second, and it slams downward pressure onto a bladder that has no say in the matter. The only thing standing between that pressure and a leak is a ring of muscle that has to contract first, hard, and slightly early. When it does not, a small amount escapes — and that is the entire mechanism.

The useful news is that the muscle is trainable and the timing is teachable, and the timing part works almost immediately.

## What a Cough Actually Does

Understanding the physics makes the fix obvious.

Coughing forces your diaphragm down and your abdominal wall in, spiking intra-abdominal pressure. That pressure transmits in every direction, including straight down onto the bladder. The bladder passes it to the urine, and the urine looks for the exit.

The exit is guarded by the urethral sphincter and, wrapped around it, the pelvic floor muscles. In a working system these **contract reflexively before the cough arrives** — your nervous system anticipates the pressure and braces for it. That pre-contraction is not something you normally think about, any more than you consciously brace your abs before lifting something heavy.

Three ways it goes wrong:

- **The muscles are too weak** to generate enough closing pressure against the spike
- **The muscles are too slow** — they contract, but after the pressure has already passed
- **The reflex has been lost**, most often after prostate surgery disrupts the local anatomy and nerve signalling

The second one is underrated. Plenty of men can hold a decent kegel for ten seconds and still leak on a cough, because a cough does not wait ten seconds. It is a power problem, not an endurance problem.

## Why It Happens to Men

**After prostate surgery.** Radical prostatectomy removes structures that contributed to continence and leaves the pelvic floor carrying more of the load alone. This is the most common cause of new stress leaking in men by a wide margin.

**Chronic cough.** A cough that runs for weeks is hundreds of pressure spikes a day, which fatigues the pelvic floor faster than it can adapt. Smoking, asthma, reflux, hay fever, and ACE inhibitor blood pressure medication are the usual culprits. Treating the cough is treating the leak.

**Age and disuse.** Pelvic floor muscle mass and speed decline with age like everything else, and virtually no man trains them until something goes wrong.

**Weight.** Extra abdominal mass raises baseline pressure on the bladder, so every cough starts from a higher number.

**Heavy lifting with bad bracing.** Repeatedly generating large intra-abdominal pressure without pelvic floor support loads the same tissue in the same direction, day after day.

If the leaking comes with sudden desperate urges rather than pressure events, that is a different problem — see [urge incontinence](/blog/urge-incontinence). If both happen, it is mixed incontinence, and the pelvic floor work helps either way.

## The Brace Technique

This is the highest-value thing in this article. Learn it today.

**The knack** means deliberately contracting the pelvic floor about one second before you cough, and holding through the whole cough.

The evidence for it is unusually clean. [Miller, Ashton-Miller, and DeLancey, in the *Journal of the American Geriatrics Society* (1998)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9670874/), taught 27 people with mild stress incontinence to pre-contract before coughing. After **one week**, leakage fell by **98.2% on a medium cough** and **73.3% on a deep cough**.

One caveat worth being straight about: that study was conducted in women. The mechanism — pre-contraction closing the urethra before the pressure spike arrives — is anatomically the same in men, and it is standard practice in male pelvic floor rehabilitation, but the trial population was female. Treat the exact percentages as illustrative rather than as your expected result.

How to do it:

1. **Feel the cough coming.** There is nearly always a fraction of a second of warning — a tickle, an inhale.
2. **Squeeze hard, right then.** Not a gentle hold. A full-strength contraction, lifting up and in.
3. **Hold through the entire cough**, including the second and third if it is a run.
4. **Release afterwards.** Do not walk around permanently clenched — a chronically tight pelvic floor causes its own problems.
5. **Repeat for sneezes, laughs, lifting, and standing up.** Same principle, any pressure spike.

For a couple of weeks you will forget, cough, and remember afterwards. That is the normal learning curve. It becomes automatic surprisingly fast once the association forms.

## Training the Muscle Underneath

The brace buys you control now. Strength is what removes the problem.

Two qualities need work, and most men only train one:

**Quick contractions.** Squeeze at full strength for one second, release completely, repeat 10 times. This trains the fast-twitch fibres that a cough demands. Full release between reps matters as much as the squeeze — a half-relaxed muscle cannot generate a full contraction.

**Long holds.** Squeeze and hold for 5 to 10 seconds, then rest for the same time, 10 times. This builds the endurance that keeps you dry across a coughing fit rather than a single cough.

**Getting the contraction right.** The action is stopping wind and lifting the testicles, at the same time. You should see a slight retraction at the base of the penis. What should *not* happen is squeezing your buttocks, tensing your thighs, sucking in your stomach, or holding your breath — those are the four ways men fake a kegel while doing nothing useful. If you are not sure you have got it, [how to tell if your pelvic floor is tight or weak](/blog/how-to-tell-if-pelvic-floor-is-tight-or-weak) walks through the checks.

Do both sets, three times a day. Ten minutes total. Consistency beats volume here by a distance — the men who fail at this almost always fail by stopping, not by training too gently.

## What the Research Says About Results

For men after prostate surgery, the benchmark is [Van Kampen and colleagues in *The Lancet* (2000)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10675166/). They randomised 102 incontinent men after radical prostatectomy to pelvic floor re-education or a control condition.

At three months, **88% of the trained group were continent versus 56% of controls.** The trained group also recovered faster and had less severe leaking along the way. The authors concluded pelvic floor re-education should be a first-line option, not a fallback after other things fail.

A realistic timeline if you are training consistently:

- **Week 1:** The brace technique starts working on the coughs you remember to prepare for. Nothing has got stronger yet.
- **Weeks 2-4:** Bracing becomes more automatic. You notice you can hold a contraction longer than at the start.
- **Weeks 6-12:** Actual strength change. Leaks get smaller and less frequent, and some coughs stop producing anything at all.
- **Beyond 12 weeks:** Continued improvement, then maintenance. Stop training and it fades — this is muscle, and detraining is real.

If twelve weeks of consistent work has produced nothing, get assessed. A pelvic floor physiotherapist can check with biofeedback whether you are contracting the right muscle at all, which is the single most common reason training fails. ## Common Questions

**Should I cut down on fluids?** No. Concentrated urine irritates the bladder and makes everything worse. Drink normally and taper in the evening if nights are an issue.

**Does stopping the flow mid-wee count as training?** Use it once to identify the muscle, then stop. Doing it regularly interferes with normal bladder emptying and can leave residual urine behind.

**Can I train while sitting at a desk?** Yes, and nobody can tell. That is one of the genuine advantages of this exercise. Standing is harder and worth progressing to, since that is when you actually leak.

**Is a pad admitting defeat?** It is managing the situation while you fix it. Use one if it keeps you living normally — just do not let it become the plan instead of the training.

**Why do I leak more at the end of the day?** Muscle fatigue. The pelvic floor has been working all day. It is a sign that endurance is the limiting factor, so weight the long holds more heavily.

**Could my blood pressure medication be causing this?** Indirectly, if it is an ACE inhibitor causing a dry persistent cough. Do not stop it — mention the cough to your doctor, since alternatives without that side effect exist.

## The Bottom Line

Weeing when you cough is a pressure spike outrunning a muscle. The brace technique fixes the timing, often within days, and it costs nothing to start. Training quick contractions alongside long holds fixes the strength behind it over six to twelve weeks.
