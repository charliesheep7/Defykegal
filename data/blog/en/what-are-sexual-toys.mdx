Sexual toys are objects designed to provide or enhance sexual stimulation, used solo or with a partner. That definition is dull on purpose, because the interesting part is not the category — it is how ordinary their use actually is, and how much of it has genuine clinical grounding.

Research published in *The Journal of Sexual Medicine* by Herbenick and colleagues found that **44.8 percent of men in the United States had incorporated a vibrator into sexual activity at some point**, with 10 percent in the past month and 14.2 percent in the past year. That is close to half of men, most of whom assume they are unusual for it.

## The Main Categories

**Vibrators and penile vibratory stimulation devices.** Anything delivering vibration to the penis, perineum, or elsewhere. This is the category with the most research behind it, largely because it crossed into clinical use.

**Strokers and sleeves.** Enclosed textured devices for solo use. Straightforward, and useful for deliberately varying stimulation patterns.

**Cock rings.** Worn at the base of the penis or around the base and scrotum. They restrict venous outflow, which maintains firmness for longer. This is a real physiological mechanism, which is exactly why they need respecting.

**Prostate massagers.** Curved devices designed to reach the prostate through the rectum. The prostate is densely innervated, and stimulation produces a distinctly different sensation from penile stimulation for many men.

**Pelvic floor and perineal trainers.** Devices using vibration or biofeedback to help locate and train the pelvic floor muscles. The closest thing to a fitness product in the category — though as we cover below, the device is rarely the bottleneck.

## What the Research Actually Shows

Two separate evidence streams are worth separating, because they get conflated constantly.

**The association.** Men who had used vibrators recently scored higher on four of the five domains of the International Index of Erectile Function — erectile function, intercourse satisfaction, orgasmic function, and sexual desire. The same [research on vibrator use and sexual health](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/06/090629100643.htm) found users were also more likely to engage in health-promoting behaviours such as testicular self-examination.

That is an association, and the direction of causation is genuinely unclear. Men who pay attention to their sexual health may simply be more likely to do both things. Nobody has shown that buying a vibrator improves erections.

**The clinical use.** This part is firmer. Penile vibratory stimulation has established application for delayed ejaculation, anejaculation, and reduced penile sensation, and is used to produce anterograde ejaculation in men with spinal cord injuries. A systematic review presented in *The Journal of Sexual Medicine* found an overwhelming majority of studies reporting positive outcomes, with no significant physical or emotional side effects reported in the literature.

For men who struggle to reach orgasm, this matters. The [Cleveland Clinic's overview of anorgasmia](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/24640-anorgasmia) lists vibratory stimulation among the practical approaches, and given that [no medication is approved to help men climax](/blog/medication-to-help-climax-male), a device with actual clinical evidence behind it deserves more attention than it gets.

Use patterns also vary meaningfully across groups — [research in *Archives of Sexual Behavior*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10508-010-9716-y) documented notably higher rates among gay and bisexual men in the United States.

## Materials: The Part That Actually Matters

Sex toys are not regulated as medical devices in most markets, which means material quality is entirely on the manufacturer. The difference between a safe toy and a bad one is not price. It is porosity.

**Use these — non-porous, cleanable, stable:**

- Medical-grade silicone
- Borosilicate glass
- Stainless steel
- ABS plastic

**Avoid these — porous, degrade over time, trap bacteria:**

- Jelly rubber
- PVC
- TPE and TPR
- Unspecified soft plastics

Porous materials cannot be fully sterilised. Bacteria sit in microscopic pores that soap and water do not reach, and the material itself breaks down with use.

The chemical concern is separate. Softeners called phthalates are used to make plastics flexible and have been linked to hormone disruption and fertility concerns. A "phthalate-free" label is weaker reassurance than it sounds, since manufacturers can substitute other softeners. Non-porous silicone, glass, or steel sidesteps the entire question.

**Cleaning is simple and non-negotiable.** Warm water and mild unscented soap before and after every use, dried fully before storage. Motor-free silicone, glass, and steel can be boiled. Never share without a fresh barrier or a full clean, and never move a toy from anal to genital contact without cleaning it first.

One more: **use the right lubricant.** Silicone lubricant degrades silicone toys. Water-based is the safe default for almost everything.

## Where Devices Help and Where They Do Not

A useful line to draw: toys are good at changing sensation. They are poor at changing physiology.

If the problem is stimulation — you cannot finish, sensation has dulled, or you want a different experience — a device addresses that directly, and the clinical evidence for vibratory stimulation supports it.

If the problem is structural, a device is the wrong tool. A cock ring compensates for venous leak; it does not improve it. Erectile difficulty that is persistent rather than occasional warrants a proper look at causes, and both the [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/erection-problems-erectile-dysfunction/) and [NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/urologic-diseases/erectile-dysfunction) are clear that ED often signals cardiovascular or metabolic issues worth investigating. The [full landscape of ED treatment](/blog/erectile-dysfunction-treatment) covers what actually moves the underlying problem.

Pelvic floor trainers sit in an interesting middle. The muscles they target — bulbospongiosus and ischiocavernosus — genuinely do matter for erection firmness and ejaculatory control, as the anatomy of the [male pelvic floor muscles](/blog/pelvic-floor-muscles) makes clear. But the device is not what produces the result; consistent progressive training is. You do not need hardware to train the pelvic floor any more than you need a machine to do push-ups, and [the standard kegel exercises for men](/blog/7-kegel-exercises-for-men) require nothing at all.

## Practical Rules for Getting Started

1. **Buy one thing, not five.** Most people use one device regularly and leave the rest in a drawer.
2. **Buy on material, not features.** Silicone, glass, or steel from a retailer that names the material explicitly. Vague listings are a bad sign.
3. **Respect the cock ring time limit.** Twenty to thirty minutes maximum, off immediately if the area becomes numb, cold, or discoloured, never worn asleep. Start with adjustable or stretchy rather than rigid metal.
4. **Start low with vibration.** High-intensity stimulation used constantly can dull sensitivity to partnered sex. Deliberately varying intensity, grip, and pace avoids conditioning yourself to a single pattern.
5. **Talk to your partner before introducing one.** Framed as an addition it usually lands well; introduced as a fix for something they are doing, rarely.

That fourth point deserves emphasis. Conditioning to a narrow, high-intensity pattern is one of the more common contributors to delayed ejaculation with a partner — the same reason varying your solo technique is standard advice. A toy used with variety helps. A toy used identically every time can work against you.

## The Bottom Line

Sexual toys are ordinary, widely used, and in the case of vibratory stimulation, backed by real clinical evidence for specific problems. Choose non-porous materials, clean them properly, respect the limits on anything that restricts blood flow, and vary how you use them.

Just keep the categories straight. Devices change sensation. Training changes the muscle. Medicine changes the physiology. Most men who are disappointed by a purchase bought it expecting the wrong one of the three.
