Search "pelvis labeled" and you'll find plenty of diagrams — but a diagram only shows you where things sit, not what they do or why you should care. For men, the pelvis isn't just a bowl of bone. It's the control center for erections, ejaculation, and bladder function. Knowing the labels turns that anatomy from a textbook picture into something you can actually train.

Here's the male pelvis, labeled part by part, in plain English — with what each structure does and why it's worth your attention.

## The bony frame, labeled

The pelvis is a ring of four bones that anchors your spine to your legs and cradles the organs inside. Working through the labels:

- **Ilium** — the large, fan-shaped upper section of each hip bone. It's the wide flare you feel when you rest your hands on your hips.
- **Ischium** — the lower, rear part of the hip bone. These are your "sit bones," the points that bear your weight when you sit.
- **Pubis** — the front section, meeting in the middle at the **pubic symphysis**, a joint of cartilage.
- **Sacrum** — the triangular bone at the base of the spine, formed from fused vertebrae, forming the back wall of the pelvis.
- **Coccyx** — the tailbone, at the very bottom of the sacrum.

The ilium, ischium, and pubis fuse into a single **hip bone** on each side. Two hip bones plus the sacrum and coccyx complete the ring. That ring matters for one reason above all: it's the frame the pelvic floor muscles attach to.

## The pelvic floor muscles, labeled

This is the layer most men have never heard named — and it's the one that runs the show. Slung across the bottom of the bony ring like a hammock, the pelvic floor is a group of muscles, not a single sheet. According to the [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/body/22729-pelvic-floor-muscles), these muscles support your bladder and bowel and wrap the structures that pass through the floor.

The labels worth knowing:

- **Levator ani** — the main muscular group, itself made of three parts:
  - **Pubococcygeus** — runs from the pubic bone to the tailbone; the primary muscle a kegel targets.
  - **Puborectalis** — slings around the rectum and helps control bowel continence.
  - **Iliococcygeus** — the broader, flatter portion that forms much of the floor's support.
- **Bulbocavernosus** — in men, this wraps the base of the penis. It helps trap blood during an erection and pumps out semen and the last drops of urine.
- **Ischiocavernosus** — runs along each side of the base of the penis and helps maintain rigidity during an erection.

Those last two are why male pelvic floor training is its own thing, not a copy of a women's program — see our [pelvic floor muscles](/blog/pelvic-floor-muscles) breakdown for more.

## What the labels mean for you

Here's the payoff of learning the map. When you do a kegel and feel the base of the penis lift and the area between it and the anus draw upward, you're feeling the levator ani and those two penile muscles contract together. That's not trivia — it's the exact mechanism behind firmer erections and better ejaculatory control.

The research backs it. In a randomized controlled trial led by physiotherapist Grace Dorey, published in [*BJU International*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16104916/), training these muscles restored normal erectile function in **40% of men and improved it in another 35.5%**. Same labels, real results.

Most men ignore this entire layer simply because they can't see it and were never taught it existed. ## How to train what you just labeled

Knowing the anatomy makes training precise. Here's the core routine, in line with the [Harvard Health step-by-step guide](https://www.health.harvard.edu/bladder-and-bowel/step-by-step-guide-to-performing-kegel-exercises):

1. **Find the levator ani.** Tighten the muscle you'd use to stop urinating or hold in gas. Keep your abs, glutes, and thighs relaxed.
2. **Squeeze and hold** for 3 to 5 seconds, then release fully for the same time.
3. **Do 4 sets of 10** across the day — 40 reps total. More than that fatigues the muscle rather than building it.
4. **Add fast squeezes** once slow holds feel easy, to train the reflex behind ejaculatory control.

## What progress looks like

Give it time. Most studies ran 8 to 16 weeks, so think in months. Early signs — firmer morning erections, less post-urination dribble, better control during sex — usually appear within 6 to 12 weeks of daily training. If instead of strength you feel tension or aching, you may be over-clenching; ease off and read [how to relax the pelvic floor muscles](/blog/how-to-relax-pelvic-floor-muscles).

## Common questions

**Is the male pelvis different from the female pelvis?** Yes. The male pelvis is generally narrower and taller, with a heart-shaped inlet, while the female pelvis is wider to allow childbirth. The muscle layout also differs around the genitals, which is why male pelvic floor training is tailored to male anatomy.

**Do I need to memorize all these labels?** No. The one that matters in practice is the levator ani — the muscle your kegel targets. The rest is useful context for understanding why training works.

**Can weak pelvic floor muscles be strengthened at any age?** Yes. Like any skeletal muscle, the pelvic floor responds to training regardless of age. If you're unsure whether yours is weak, our [weak pelvic floor symptoms](/blog/weak-pelvic-floor-symptoms) guide helps you assess it.
