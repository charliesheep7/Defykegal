You think about your bladder several times a day, but probably have no idea what it actually looks like. That matters more than it seems. Once you can picture it, symptoms like urgency, leaks or a feeling of not emptying start to make sense.

**The bladder is a stretchy, pink, balloon-like muscle that sits low in your pelvis and is held shut by a ring of muscles you can train.** Here is what it looks like, how it is built and how it works with your pelvic floor.

## The Short Answer: Shape, Color and Size

According to [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/body/25010-bladder), the bladder is a hollow, roughly spherical organ that is pink or dark pink.

Its size changes a lot with how full it is:

- **Empty:** about 2 inches across
- **Full:** it can stretch to about 6 inches
- **Capacity:** about 500 to 700 mL for most people, or roughly two cups

The [NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/urologic-diseases/urinary-tract-how-it-works) describes it as a hollow, muscular, balloon-shaped organ that expands as it fills, and says a normal bladder holds about 1.5 to 2 cups. The comparison to a balloon is apt. **As it fills, the bladder wall stretches, and when you pee it shrinks back down.**

## Where the Bladder Sits

The bladder lives in the lower part of your belly, inside the pelvis between the hip bones. Ligaments connect it to nearby organs and to the pelvis, which keeps it in place.

Its neighbors depend on your anatomy. Cleveland Clinic notes that in males it rests between the pubic bone at the front and the rectum at the back. In females, it sits in front of the vagina and uterus.

In men, the prostate wraps around the urethra just below the bladder. That is why an enlarged prostate so often changes how you pee. For a wider view of the region, see our guide to [pelvic anatomy](/blog/pelvic-anatomy).

## The Four Parts of the Bladder

Cleveland Clinic breaks the bladder into four regions:

1. **Dome.** Also called the apex, this is the top front part, pointing toward your belly wall.
2. **Body.** The main section between the dome and the base.
3. **Base.** The bottom back part, also called the fundus.
4. **Neck.** A narrow band of muscle along the base that connects to the urethra.

The neck is where storage turns into release. It is part of the gate that keeps urine in until you choose to let it out.

## The Three Layers of the Bladder Wall

If you could cut through the bladder wall, you would see three layers:

- **Urothelium.** The inner lining that stops urine from leaking into the body.
- **Lamina propria.** A thin layer of loose connective tissue that carries blood vessels and nerves.
- **Muscularis propria.** The thick outer muscle layer, made of three layers of smooth muscle.

That outer layer is often called the detrusor. **Because it is smooth muscle, it tightens and relaxes automatically.** You cannot flex it on command the way you can flex a bicep. For more on how that muscle behaves, see our guide to the [bladder muscle](/blog/bladder-muscle).

## How the Bladder Fills and Empties

The bladder is one part of a team. The NIDDK explains that your kidneys filter about 120 to 150 quarts of blood a day and produce about 1 to 2 quarts of urine. Two thin tubes called ureters carry that urine down to the bladder, where it waits until you empty it through the urethra.

While the bladder fills, its muscular wall stays relaxed and the sphincter muscles stay tight. Cleveland Clinic says that when it holds about 200 to 350 mL, nerves in the bladder tell your brain it is time to find a toilet.

When you go, the sequence flips. Your brain signals the sphincters to relax, then tells the bladder wall to squeeze, pushing urine out through the urethra.

Holding on too long strains that system. Our guide to [what happens when you hold your pee](/blog/what-happens-when-you-hold-your-pee) explains why.

## Where Your Pelvic Floor Comes In

Here is the part most people never learn. The NIDDK describes two sets of sphincter muscles that close off the urethra:

- **The internal sphincter** at the bladder neck and urethra stays closed until your brain signals you to pee. It is not under conscious control.
- **The external sphincter** surrounds the internal one and adds extra pressure to keep the urethra shut.

**The NIDDK notes that you can consciously squeeze the external sphincter and the pelvic floor muscles to keep urine from leaking.** That is exactly what a kegel trains.

The evidence for that training is strong. A [2018 Cochrane review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30288727/) by Dumoulin and colleagues pooled 31 trials with 1,817 women. Women with stress incontinence who did pelvic floor muscle training were eight times more likely to report a cure than those who had no treatment, at 56 percent versus 6 percent. The review studied women, but men use the same external sphincter and pelvic floor muscles to hold urine.

## Signs Something Is Wrong With Your Bladder

Cleveland Clinic lists common signs of a bladder problem:

- Loss of bladder control
- Burning or pain when peeing
- Cloudy or bloody urine
- Peeing more often than is normal for you
- Feeling like you cannot fully empty
- Strong or foul-smelling urine
- Pain during sex

Common bladder conditions include cystitis, the most common type of urinary tract infection, as well as overactive bladder, incontinence, bladder stones and bladder cancer. If urgency is your main issue, start with our guide to [overactive bladder](/blog/overactive-bladder).

**Blood in your urine, pain or a sudden change in how you pee are worth a doctor's visit, not a wait-and-see.** Doctors can look at the bladder directly with tests like ultrasound or cystoscopy, a thin camera passed through the urethra.

## How to Keep Your Bladder Healthy

A few habits go a long way:

1. **Drink enough water.** Cleveland Clinic notes that water helps flush waste and bacteria out of the urinary tract, and concentrated urine can irritate the bladder.
2. **Do not hold it for hours.** Go when you feel the urge rather than pushing it.
3. **Train your pelvic floor.** Short daily kegel sessions strengthen the muscles you use to hold urine in.
4. **Get symptoms checked.** Leaks, pain or blood are signals, not things to put up with.

## The Bottom Line

The bladder is a pink, balloon-like muscle about 2 inches across when empty that can stretch to around 6 inches when full. It has four parts and three layers, and its wall works automatically. The muscles that hold it shut, especially the external sphincter and pelvic floor, are the part you can train. That makes a daily kegel habit one of the simplest ways to look after it.
