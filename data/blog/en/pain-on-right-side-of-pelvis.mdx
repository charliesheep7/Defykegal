Pain on the right side of the pelvis is one of those symptoms that can mean something minor or something urgent, and the location alone does not tell you which. A strained groin muscle and appendicitis can start in almost the same spot.

What separates them is the pattern: how the pain started, what makes it worse, and what else is happening in your body. Once you know what to look for, most causes sort themselves into "watch it", "book an appointment" or "go now".

This guide covers the common causes in men, how each one tends to behave, and the red flags that should never wait.

## Red flags: get help now

Start here, because some causes need treatment within hours.

**Go to A&E or call emergency services if you have:**

- Severe abdominal or pelvic pain that is getting worse
- Pain that started around your belly button and moved to the lower right
- Fever, vomiting or feeling very unwell alongside the pain
- A painful, hard bulge in the groin that will not go back in
- Sudden, severe pain in a testicle, with or without swelling
- Pain with blood in your urine and a high temperature

If none of those apply, keep reading. Most right-sided pelvic pain has a less dramatic explanation.

## What sits on the right side of the pelvis

It helps to know what is actually there. The pelvis is a ring of bone housing the bladder, the end of the bowel and the prostate, with the pelvic floor muscles forming the base. Our [map of where the pelvis is located](/blog/where-is-the-pelvis-located) goes into more detail.

On the right side specifically, you have:

- **The appendix** and the start of the large bowel
- **The right ureter**, the tube carrying urine from the right kidney to the bladder
- **The inguinal canal**, a natural weak point in the abdominal wall above the groin
- **The right hip joint** and the muscles that cross it
- **Half of the pelvic floor**, which can tighten more on one side than the other

Each of those can hurt, and each tends to hurt in its own way.

## The common causes, and how they feel

### 1. Appendicitis

This is the one to rule out. According to the [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/appendicitis/), appendicitis pain usually **starts in the middle of the abdomen around the belly button, then moves to the lower right side over a few hours and gets worse**. It is often worse when you move, cough or press on the area.

Nausea, loss of appetite and a raised temperature commonly come with it. A US analysis by Addiss and colleagues in the [American Journal of Epidemiology](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2239906/) estimated the **lifetime risk of appendicitis at 8.6% for men**, higher than for women. It needs hospital treatment, because an untreated appendix can burst.

### 2. Inguinal hernia

A hernia happens when part of the bowel or fatty tissue pushes through the weak spot in the groin. It typically causes a **bulge that grows when you cough, strain or stand**, and shrinks when you lie down. It may ache or feel heavy, particularly by the end of the day.

This is very much a male problem. A large Oxford study by Primatesta and Goldacre in the [International Journal of Epidemiology](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8921464/) estimated the **lifetime risk of hernia repair at 27% for men and 3% for women**. Most hernias are not urgent, but a bulge that becomes painful, firm and will not go back in can be trapped. The [NHS guide to inguinal hernia repair](https://www.nhs.uk/tests-and-treatments/inguinal-hernia-repair/) explains what treatment involves.

### 3. Kidney stone

A stone moving down the right ureter causes pain that often **starts in the side or back and travels toward the groin**. It tends to come in waves — intense, then easing, then intense again — and can make it hard to sit still.

The [NHS kidney stones page](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/kidney-stones/) notes stones affect more than 1 in 10 people and most often appear between 30 and 60. Blood in the urine, nausea and needing to pee often are common. Fever alongside a stone needs urgent attention.

### 4. Muscle strain

In active men, this is often the answer. Strains of the **adductors** (inner thigh) or **hip flexors** cause pain at the front of the pelvis and groin that is clearly linked to movement: sprinting, kicking, twisting, or getting out of a car.

The pain eases with rest, there is no fever or nausea, and you can usually recall the moment it started. Most strains settle over a few weeks with relative rest and a gradual return to activity.

### 5. Hip joint problems

Hip joint issues, including early arthritis and labral tears, often cause deep groin pain rather than pain at the side of the hip. It is typically worse with deep bending, sitting for a long time or rotating the leg, and may come with clicking or stiffness.

### 6. Bowel causes

Constipation, trapped wind, irritable bowel syndrome and inflammatory bowel disease can all cause pain low on the right. The clue is a link to eating, bowel movements or bloating.

### 7. A tight pelvic floor

This one is easy to miss. An overactive, or hypertonic, pelvic floor can cause aching in the groin, lower abdomen, perineum or rectum — and it does not always feel even on both sides.

The pattern is different from the causes above. It usually **builds gradually, flares with stress or long periods of sitting, and comes without fever or a clear injury**. It may come with urinary urgency, discomfort after ejaculation, or a feeling of pressure. Our guides to [a hypertonic pelvic floor](/blog/hypertonic-pelvic-floor) and [pelvic pain in men](/blog/pelvic-pain) explain why this is often mistaken for infection.

## Why kegels are not the first answer

It is tempting to assume pelvic pain means weak muscles that need strengthening. Often it is the opposite.

If your pain comes from a pelvic floor that is already clenched, adding more squeezes can make it worse. The first job is to find out what is causing the pain. If a tight pelvic floor is part of it, the next job is learning to let go.

A pelvic floor physiotherapist can assess this directly. In the meantime, gentle [pelvic floor relaxation](/blog/how-to-relax-pelvic-floor-muscles) is low-risk:

1. Lie on your back with your knees bent and feet flat.
2. Breathe slowly into your belly so it rises on the in-breath.
3. On each in-breath, let the area between your sit bones soften and drop, as if you are about to pee.
4. Do not push. Just release.
5. Repeat for five minutes a day.

Once the pain has settled and the muscles can relax fully, balanced strengthening becomes useful again — for bladder control, erections and ejaculatory control.

## Getting a diagnosis

For pain that is not an emergency but has lasted more than a few days, or keeps returning, book an appointment. Useful things to note before you go:

- Where exactly it hurts and whether the pain moves
- When it started and whether anything triggered it
- What makes it better or worse
- Any urinary, bowel or sexual symptoms
- Fever, weight loss or night sweats

Depending on the pattern, your doctor may examine your abdomen and groin, test your urine, or arrange an ultrasound or CT scan. For recurring aches without a clear structural cause, our article on [an ache in the pelvis area](/blog/ache-in-pelvis-area) covers the chronic causes in more depth.

## What recovery looks like

Recovery depends entirely on the cause. Appendicitis and a trapped hernia are treated surgically, and most men are back to normal activity within a few weeks. A kidney stone often passes on its own over days to weeks.

Muscle strains usually improve within two to six weeks. Pelvic floor tension typically responds over one to three months of relaxation work and physiotherapy, with gradual rather than overnight change.

If pain is not improving on the expected timeline, go back to your doctor rather than pushing through.

## Common questions

**Can stress cause pain on the right side of the pelvis?** Stress does not cause appendicitis or hernias, but it can tighten the pelvic floor and worsen bowel symptoms, both of which cause real pain.

**Is right-sided pelvic pain linked to the prostate?** Prostate pain is usually felt centrally, in the perineum or rectum, rather than clearly on one side.

**Can sitting too long cause it?** Long sitting can aggravate hip problems and pelvic floor tension. Regular breaks help.

## The short version

Pain on the right side of the pelvis in men ranges from a strained groin muscle to appendicitis. Pain that moves to the lower right, a bulge that will not go back in, sudden testicle pain, fever or vomiting all need urgent help.

For slower, stress-linked aching without fever, a tight pelvic floor is a strong possibility — and relaxing comes before strengthening.
