If you want to locate your pubic bone, the fastest way is to lie down, put two fingers on your navel and walk them straight down the midline. The first firm ridge of bone you meet, sitting just above the genitals, is the front of your pubic bone.

That takes about thirty seconds. The more useful question is why you would bother — and the answer is that this bone is the front anchor of your entire pelvic floor. Once you can find it, kegel exercises stop being a guessing game.

## What the Pubic Bone Actually Is

Your pelvis is built from two hip bones joined to the sacrum at the back. Each hip bone has three parts that fuse during adolescence: the ilium (the wide flare you rest your hands on), the ischium (what you sit on), and the **pubis** at the front and bottom.

The two pubic bones meet in the midline at a joint called the **pubic symphysis** — a disc of fibrocartilage wrapped in dense ligament. It is deliberately stiff. Under normal conditions it permits only a couple of millimetres of movement, which is what makes it such a dependable landmark: it does not wander.

Together these form the ring of the pelvic girdle. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/body/pelvis) and [TeachMeAnatomy](https://teachmeanatomy.info/pelvis/bones/pelvic-girdle/) both have clear breakdowns of how the pieces fit if you want the full picture. Our own [pelvic anatomy](/blog/pelvic-anatomy) explainer covers the same ground in plainer terms.

## Finding It, Step by Step

1. **Lie on your back with knees bent.** This is the single biggest factor. Standing or sitting keeps the abdominal wall tense and buries the bone under muscle.
2. **Start at your navel.** Place two or three fingertips there.
3. **Walk downward in the midline.** Move in small steps, pressing gently and slowly rather than poking. You are travelling through soft abdominal wall.
4. **Feel for the change.** Somewhere around a hand's width below the navel — the exact distance varies with height and build — the soft tissue gives way to a firm, unyielding horizontal ridge. That is the upper border of the pubic bone.
5. **Trace sideways.** Run your fingers outward from the midline along that ridge. It curves away from you on both sides toward your hips, confirming you have bone and not a tense muscle band.
6. **Find the midline seam.** Press gently at the centre point. That slight central notch is the pubic symphysis.

If you cannot feel anything firm, do not press harder. A softer, slower approach with fully relaxed abdominal muscles works far better than force. Body composition genuinely changes how easy this is, and that is normal.

## Why This Landmark Matters

Your pelvic floor is a sheet of muscle slung between the pubic bone at the front and the coccyx at the back, spanning side to side between the sitting bones. The main muscle group, the levator ani, includes a portion called the **pubococcygeus** — named directly after its two attachment points, pubis and coccyx.

That geometry tells you what a correct contraction feels like. A kegel is not a squeeze and it is not a push. It is a **lift inward and upward**, toward the pubic bone and toward your head. Once you know where the front anchor sits, that direction stops being abstract.

The landmark also explains a common error. Many people bear down instead of lifting, producing downward pressure through the same tissue. If you rest a hand just above the pubic bone during a contraction, you should feel a subtle drawing-in — never a bulge pushing out against your hand. Our guide to [pelvic floor muscles](/blog/pelvic-floor-muscles) goes deeper on the anatomy, and [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/body/22729-pelvic-floor-muscles) covers the muscle group in clinical terms.

## Putting It to Work

Use the bone as a feedback point while you learn the contraction:

1. **Set up.** Lie on your back, knees bent, one hand flat on your lower abdomen just above the pubic bone.
2. **Find the muscle.** Imagine stopping the flow of urine and holding back wind at the same time. Aim the effort up and in, toward the hand resting above your pubic bone.
3. **Check your hand.** Your abdomen should stay soft and still. A hard bulge means you are bracing your abs or bearing down.
4. **Hold 5, release 5.** Ten slow repetitions. The release is half the exercise — let go completely.
5. **Add ten quick lifts.** Fast on, fast off. This trains the reflex that stops a leak when you cough or sneeze.
6. **Three short sessions a day.** Under three minutes total. [NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/urologic-diseases/kegel-exercises) describes a comparable schedule.

Breathe throughout. Holding your breath drives pressure downward through the pelvic floor, which works directly against what you are trying to build.

## When the Pubic Bone Hurts

Pain at the pubic bone or symphysis has a handful of common explanations:

- **Osteitis pubis.** Inflammation at the symphysis from repetitive loading. Common in footballers, runners and anyone doing high volumes of kicking or cutting.
- **Pelvic girdle pain in pregnancy.** Hormonal softening of the ligaments increases movement at a joint built to stay still. Often felt as sharp pain when rolling in bed or climbing stairs.
- **Adductor strain.** The groin muscles attach nearby, and a tear can feel like bone pain.
- **Referred pelvic floor pain.** An overactive pelvic floor can produce aching around its attachment points. If urinating starts slowly or you feel constantly clenched, see [how to tell if your pelvic floor is tight or weak](/blog/how-to-tell-if-pelvic-floor-is-tight-or-weak) before adding more kegels.
- **Stress fracture.** Less common, but worth ruling out after a sharp increase in training load.

Get it assessed if the pain persists beyond a couple of weeks, follows a fall, wakes you at night, or comes with fever.

## What to Expect Next

Finding the bone is the easy part and takes one attempt. Learning to reliably lift toward it without recruiting your glutes, thighs or abdominal wall usually takes one to two weeks of short daily practice.

From there, most people notice better bladder control between weeks four and six, with fuller strength gains around three months. If you are still unsure whether you are contracting correctly after a few weeks, a pelvic health physiotherapist can confirm it in a single appointment — technique, not effort, is the most common reason pelvic floor training fails.
