Nobody forgets how to do a kegel. They forget to do it. That is the entire problem a reminder image is trying to solve, and most of the ones floating around online solve the wrong half of it — they show an anatomical cross-section of the pelvic floor, which tells you what the muscle is and nothing about what to do in the next thirty seconds.

A reminder that works is closer to a gym whiteboard than a textbook diagram. Hold time. Rep count. The moment that triggers it. That is it.

## Why the Reminder Matters More Than the Technique

The technique is not complicated, and a week of practice gets most men reasonably good at it. What separates the men who see change at week 12 from the men who do not is how many of those 84 days actually happened.

That is measurable. In a 2023 assessor-blind randomised controlled trial by Kijmanawat and colleagues, published in the *Journal of Clinical Medicine*, participants doing pelvic floor muscle training with an app that included a vibration and sound reminder reached [59.1% adherence over 12 weeks versus 37.8% in the control group](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10672388/). The reminder group also reported a significantly larger reduction in daily incontinence episodes and better quality-of-life scores. The trial was run in women with stress urinary incontinence, but the adherence mechanism is not sex-specific — pelvic floor training fails for the same reason in everyone, which is that it is invisible, boring and easy to postpone.

The habit research points the same way. [Gardner, Lally and Wardle, writing in the *British Journal of General Practice* in 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3505409/), described habits as actions "triggered automatically in response to contextual cues," and reported that automaticity plateaued on average around **66 days** after the first daily performance. Two months of deliberate prompting before the behaviour carries itself.

## What to Put on the Image

Strip it down. Four elements, in this order of prominence:

1. **The cue.** The specific moment that triggers the set — "after brushing teeth," "every red light," "when the kettle goes on." This is the most important line on the card and should be the biggest text.
2. **The hold.** A single number in seconds. Not a range. Ranges invite negotiation.
3. **The reps.** One number again. Ten is plenty for a single set.
4. **A one-line form check.** "Lift, do not squeeze. Abs soft. Breathe."

What to leave off: anatomy illustrations, motivational slogans, medical explanations, and anything about ED or incontinence. A reminder you would rather not have someone else see is a reminder you will end up taking down.

Here is the text of a card that works, at three stages of training:

| Stage | Cue | Hold | Reps | Sets per day |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Weeks 1–2 | After brushing teeth | 3 sec | 5 | 2 |
| Weeks 3–6 | After brushing teeth + commute | 5 sec | 8 | 3 |
| Weeks 7–12 | Same, plus one evening set | 10 sec | 10 | 3 |

Update the card when the numbers change. A reminder showing week-one numbers in week eight quietly teaches you that the card is out of date and can be ignored.

## Where to Put It

Placement decides everything. The best spot is not the most visible one — it is the one attached to a behaviour you have never skipped in your adult life.

- **Phone lock screen.** Highest exposure by a wide margin. Set the card as your wallpaper and you see it forty times a day.
- **Bathroom mirror.** Pairs with brushing teeth, which is already a twice-daily fixed routine.
- **Inside a wardrobe or medicine cabinet door.** Private, and tied to getting dressed.
- **Car dashboard or steering column.** Red lights are dead time and a genuinely good place to train, since nobody can tell you are doing it.
- **Desk monitor bezel.** Works if you have a private workspace, less so in an open office.

Avoid the fridge, the front door and anywhere else with heavy household traffic. Not because there is anything to hide, but because a reminder you feel self-conscious about lasts about ten days.

## The Routine the Image Should Trigger

A reminder is worthless if the thing it prompts is vague. Here is the set, in full, so the card only has to jog your memory rather than teach you.

1. **Find the muscle.** Lift the base of the penis and draw the testicles slightly upward. The [NIDDK's guidance on locating the pelvic floor muscles](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/urologic-diseases/kegel-exercises) is the plainest description of the sensation. If you still cannot feel it clearly, [how to tell if your pelvic floor is tight or weak](/blog/how-to-tell-if-pelvic-floor-is-tight-or-weak) is the better starting point.
2. **Contract for the hold time on the card.** Count it out. Do not guess — most men undercount by two or three seconds once the muscle fatigues.
3. **Release completely.** Full release is half the exercise. Rest as long as you held.
4. **Repeat for the rep count.** If the last two reps get shaky or your glutes join in, stop there and drop the hold time on your card by a second.
5. **Breathe normally throughout.** Breath-holding creates downward abdominal pressure that works directly against the contraction.

Three sets a day at these numbers takes under five minutes total. That is the whole time commitment, which is exactly why forgetting rather than difficulty is the failure mode.

## Four Ways Reminder Images Fail

1. **Too much information.** A card with anatomy, benefits, technique notes and a schedule is a poster, not a prompt. You will read it twice and then stop seeing it.
2. **No cue attached.** "Do your kegels" with no trigger is a suggestion. "After you brush your teeth" is an instruction with a built-in start time.
3. **Never refreshed.** Visual cues go stale — the brain stops registering an image that has not moved in six weeks. Change the placement or redesign the card every month or so.
4. **Placed where you feel watched.** Self-consciousness kills adherence faster than boredom. Private placement, every time.

## When to Stop Needing It

Two months of daily repetition is the realistic threshold, per the habit-formation research above. Some men get there in three weeks; some need four months. The signal is not a date on a calendar — it is noticing you have already done the set before you saw the card.

Even then, keep something in place. Maintenance training is one set a day at your final hold time, three or four days a week, and that is precisely the volume that quietly disappears when the prompt goes away. For a full week-by-week progression to put on the card, [the 12-week printable kegel plan](/blog/kegel-men-pdf) has the whole table, and [kegel exercise pictures for men](/blog/kegel-exercise-pictures-for-man) covers the visual side of the technique itself.

One warning worth taking seriously: if daily training makes urgency, pelvic aching or difficulty relaxing worse instead of better, more reminders will make it worse still. That pattern usually means a [hypertonic pelvic floor](/blog/hypertonic-pelvic-floor) — a muscle that is too tight rather than too weak — and it needs the opposite approach.

## The Short Version

Make the card small. Put the cue in the biggest text, the hold and rep numbers underneath, and nothing else. Stick it somewhere private that is welded to a daily routine you never skip. Update the numbers as they change, and expect to need it for about two months.

The reminder is not the training. It is just the thing that makes the training happen on day 47, which is the day the outcome is actually decided.
