## The Muscles Most Men Never Think About

The male pelvic floor is a group of muscles between the pubic bone and tailbone. Its deeper layer supports the bladder and bowel; muscles closer to the base of the penis help with urinary control, erections, and ejaculation. These muscles need to contract **and** fully relax.

This page is an anatomy map: which muscles do what, where to feel a contraction, and which symptoms should prompt an assessment. For a broader introduction, read [what the pelvic floor is](/blog/what-is-the-pelvic-floor). For a step-by-step routine, use the [pelvic floor muscles workout](/blog/pelvic-floor-muscles-workout).

Pelvic symptoms and erectile difficulties have many possible causes. A muscle exercise program may help some men, but the symptoms alone cannot tell you whether the muscles are weak, overly tense, or functioning normally.

## What the Pelvic Floor Actually Is

The **pelvic floor** is a layered sheet of muscle slung like a hammock across the bottom of your pelvis, running from the pubic bone at the front to the tailbone at the back. It closes off the base of the torso, supporting everything above it while allowing controlled passage for urine, stool, and semen.

In men, it is organized into layers, according to [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK482200/) and male pelvic anatomy references:

- **The deep layer — the levator ani.** This is the large, broad muscle group that forms the main floor: the pubococcygeus, puborectalis, and iliococcygeus, plus the coccygeus behind them. Together they support the pelvic organs and maintain continence.
- **The superficial layer.** Sitting closer to the surface, around the base of the penis, are the **ischiocavernosus** and **bulbospongiosus** muscles. These are the ones most directly involved in erections and ejaculation.

| Muscle or group | Where it sits | Main contribution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Levator ani** (including puborectalis) | Deep inside the pelvis, from the pubic region toward the tailbone | Supports pelvic organs and helps control the bowel and bladder outlets |
| **Coccygeus** | Behind the levator ani, toward the tailbone | Adds support to the back of the pelvic floor |
| **Bulbospongiosus** | Around the bulb at the base of the penis | Helps expel the last drops of urine and contracts during ejaculation |
| **Ischiocavernosus** | Along the two crura at the base of the penis | Helps maintain erection rigidity by compressing blood outflow |

The [Cleveland Clinic anatomy overview](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/body/22729-pelvic-floor-muscles) describes the pelvic floor's support and continence roles. The [Oxford University Hospitals guide for men](https://www.ouh.nhs.uk/media/in3ekdyl/74150guide.pdf) shows where the muscles sit and how a correct contraction may look and feel. Nerve and blood vessel function also matter; muscle strength is only one part of the picture.

## The Three Jobs They Do

For all the anatomy, the pelvic floor really has three jobs.

### Job 1: Support

The deep levator ani group supports the bladder and bowel and responds when you cough, lift, or bear down. Weakness can contribute to leakage, but urinary symptoms can also come from the bladder, prostate, nerves, or a pelvic floor that has difficulty relaxing.

### Job 2: Continence

The pelvic floor helps the urethral and anal sphincters close and relax at the right times. The bulbospongiosus helps clear the last drops of urine from the urethra. If you leak when coughing or notice after-dribble, these muscles may be involved, but a clinician can help identify the cause. Our guide on [overactive bladder](/blog/overactive-bladder) covers a different pattern of bladder symptoms.

### Job 3: Sexual Function

An erection depends on blood flow, nerves, hormones, and arousal. The **ischiocavernosus and bulbospongiosus muscles can help limit blood outflow** at the base of the penis and support rigidity; they do not determine erection quality on their own.

During ejaculation, these muscles also contract rhythmically. Pelvic floor training may help some men with erectile or ejaculatory difficulties, but persistent symptoms deserve a medical assessment to check for other causes.

## Why They Weaken — and Why It Matters

The pelvic floor can be affected by several factors. Common contributors to weakness or poor coordination include:

- **Age or injury** — muscle and nerve function may change over time
- **Prostate surgery** — radical prostatectomy can disrupt pelvic floor function, which is why pelvic floor training is a standard part of recovery
- **Chronic straining** — from constipation or heavy lifting with poor mechanics
- **Excess weight** — added downward pressure over time

Pain, urgency, and difficulty starting a urine stream can also occur when the pelvic floor is too tense. If those fit your symptoms, read [how to tell whether your pelvic floor is tight or weak](/blog/how-to-tell-if-pelvic-floor-is-tight-or-weak) and seek an assessment before adding more contractions.

## How to Train Them

You train the pelvic floor with **kegel exercises** — deliberately contracting and relaxing these muscles, the same ones you would use to stop urine mid-stream or hold back gas. The technique is the same one [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/treatments/14611-kegel-exercises) outlines, adapted for male anatomy.

A simple starting routine:

1. **Find the muscles.** Imagine stopping your urine flow and holding back gas at the same time. That lift-and-squeeze sensation is your pelvic floor. (Use the stop-urine trick only to locate them, not as a regular exercise.)
2. **Contract and lift** the muscles, hold for 3 to 5 seconds, keeping your abs, glutes, and thighs relaxed.
3. **Fully release** for the same duration — the relaxation phase matters as much as the squeeze.
4. **Repeat** for 8 to 12 reps, a few times a day.
5. **Progress gradually**, adding longer holds and quick "flick" contractions as you get stronger.

Correct form and full relaxation matter more than a fixed rep target. Stop if pain or urinary symptoms worsen. Our guides on [pelvic floor therapy](/blog/pelvic-floor-therapy) and [pelvic floor dysfunction](/blog/pelvic-floor-dysfunction) cover assessment and troubleshooting in more detail.

In a [2004 trial of 55 men with erectile dysfunction](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15527607/), pelvic floor exercises with biofeedback improved erectile-function scores compared with a control group after three months. Following the study's treatment and crossover period, 40% reported normal erectile function and 34.5% reported improvement. That result is encouraging, but it does not mean the same response or timeline is expected for everyone.

## What Progress Looks Like

It can take several weeks to learn the right contraction and notice a change. The pace depends on the symptom, its cause, and whether the exercise is appropriate. The [Oxford University Hospitals guide](https://www.ouh.nhs.uk/media/in3ekdyl/74150guide.pdf) recommends progressing from a comfortable starting point and seeking individual guidance from a pelvic-health physiotherapist when needed.

If you are unsure whether you are contracting and releasing correctly, or symptoms persist despite practice, ask a clinician or pelvic-health physiotherapist to check your technique and possible causes.

## Common Questions

**Are pelvic floor muscles the same as core muscles?**
They are related but not identical. The pelvic floor forms the bottom of the core "canister," working with the deep abdominals and diaphragm. Training the pelvic floor specifically targets functions — erections, continence — that general core work does not reach.

**Can pelvic floor muscles be too tight?**
Yes. An overly tight or hypertonic pelvic floor can cause pain, urgency, and difficulty relaxing or emptying the bladder. If you have these symptoms, see our guides on [pelvic pain](/blog/pelvic-pain) and [pelvic floor relaxation](/blog/how-to-relax-pelvic-floor-muscles), and consider a professional assessment before doing more kegels.

**How long until I feel a difference?**
There is no reliable timeline for every person. Coordination may improve over several weeks; symptom changes depend on the cause and the treatment plan.

**Do I need equipment?**
No. Kegels require nothing but your own muscles. A guided program helps with form, consistency, and progression — see the [Defy support page](/support) for how the app structures it.

## The Bottom Line

The male pelvic floor is a layered group of muscles that supports organs, helps control urine and bowel function, and contributes to sexual function. The anatomy is useful because it tells you what can be trained — and why a symptom does not, by itself, identify the cause.

If strengthening is appropriate for you, practice a gentle lift and a complete release. If you have pain, trouble emptying your bladder, or persistent erectile difficulty, get individual advice before following a routine.
