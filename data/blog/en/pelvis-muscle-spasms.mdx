A sudden clench deep behind your scrotum. A stabbing pain in your rectum that wakes you at night. An ache at the base of your penis after sex. Pelvis muscle spasms are more common in men than most people realise, and they are often mistaken for a prostate problem.

The key thing to understand: **a spasm is a muscle that cannot let go.** That is why the usual advice — do more kegels — can make things worse.

Here is what is happening, why it happens, and the relaxation-first approach that helps most men.

## What pelvis muscle spasms actually are

Your pelvic floor is a hammock of muscles stretching from the pubic bone to the tailbone. It supports your bladder and bowel, controls urine and stool, and helps with erections and ejaculation.

Like any muscle, it can cramp. According to [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/14459-pelvic-floor-dysfunction), pelvic floor dysfunction includes muscles that tighten when they should relax, which can cause pain, urinary problems and constipation.

Spasms usually show up in one of two ways:

- **Sudden, short attacks.** Proctalgia fugax is a sharp pain in the anus or rectum. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/proctalgia-fugax) says it can last from a few seconds up to 30 minutes, and researchers estimate up to 18% of people experience it.
- **Ongoing tension.** A pelvic floor that stays tight most of the time, often called a [hypertonic pelvic floor](/blog/hypertonic-pelvic-floor), causes a dull ache that flares with sitting, stress or sex.

## Common symptoms in men

Pelvic muscle spasms can feel like:

- Cramping or stabbing pain between the scrotum and anus (the perineum)
- Rectal pain or a feeling of sitting on a golf ball
- Pain in the testicles, penis tip or groin
- **Pain during or after ejaculation**
- A slow, hesitant or stop-start urine stream
- Feeling like you have not fully emptied your bladder or bowel
- Pain that gets worse with long periods of sitting

## Why the pelvic floor spasms

There is rarely a single cause. The most common triggers include:

1. **Stress and anxiety.** Many men unconsciously clench their pelvic floor when stressed, the same way others clench their jaw.
2. **Long hours sitting**, especially on hard seats or bikes.
3. **Constipation and straining**, which overload the muscles.
4. **Over-training.** Doing lots of kegels without relaxing between reps.
5. **Holding urine or stool** for long periods.
6. **A past infection, injury or surgery** that leaves muscles guarding against pain.

### The prostatitis connection

Many men with pelvic spasms are told they have chronic prostatitis. But while short-term prostatitis is usually caused by infection, the [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/prostatitis/) notes that it is not always known what causes the long-term, chronic kind.

Research points to the muscles. In the Chronic Prostatitis Cohort Study, published in the [Journal of Urology](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18082223/), **51% of men with chronic pelvic pain syndrome** had tenderness on examination, compared with only **7% of men without symptoms**. Tenderness in the pelvic floor muscles was found in these men and in none of the controls, and it did not correlate with infection or inflammation.

In other words, for a lot of men, the pain is coming from tight, irritated muscle, not the prostate.

## How to relieve pelvic muscle spasms

The goal is to teach the muscle to let go. Work through these steps in order.

### 1. Stop the squeeze

If you are doing kegels and have pain, pause them for now. Notice when you clench during the day — at your desk, driving, lifting, or when anxious — and consciously let the area drop.

### 2. Breathe into your belly

Lie on your back with knees bent. Breathe in slowly through your nose so your belly rises. As you inhale, imagine your pelvic floor gently lowering and widening. Exhale slowly and let it rise back on its own. Do this for 5 minutes, twice a day.

### 3. Stretch the hips and pelvic floor

Gentle stretches take pressure off the muscles:

- **Happy baby:** lie on your back, grab your feet and let your knees fall wide
- **Child's pose:** kneel and sit back on your heels with arms stretched forward
- **Deep squat:** hold a supported squat with heels down for 30-60 seconds

Our guide to [pelvic floor stretches](/blog/pelvic-floor-stretches) has more options.

### 4. Use heat

A warm bath, sitz bath or heat pack on the perineum or lower belly relaxes the muscles, especially during a flare.

### 5. Fix constipation

More fibre, more water and not straining take a daily load off the pelvic floor.

### 6. Get pelvic floor physical therapy

For persistent spasms, specialist physical therapy has the best evidence. In a randomised trial published in the [Journal of Urology](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19535099/), **57% of patients** with urologic chronic pelvic pain responded to myofascial physical therapy, compared with **21%** who received general massage.

A separate study at Stanford combined trigger point release with relaxation training in 138 men with chronic pelvic pain who had not improved on standard treatment. In the [Journal of Urology](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15947608/) report, **72%** rated themselves moderately or markedly improved. Read more about [what pelvic floor physical therapy involves](/blog/pelvic-floor-physical-therapy).

## When kegels come back in

Once spasms settle, gentle pelvic floor training can help rebuild normal, balanced control. The rule is simple: **the relax phase must be at least as long as the squeeze.**

1. Squeeze gently at about 50% effort for 3 seconds.
2. Relax completely for 6 seconds, feeling the muscle drop.
3. Repeat 5-10 times.
4. Stop if pain returns.

If you are unsure whether your muscles are tight or weak, our guide on [how to tell if your pelvic floor is tight or weak](/blog/how-to-tell-if-pelvic-floor-is-tight-or-weak) walks through the signs.

## What progress looks like

- **Sudden spasms** like proctalgia fugax often pass on their own within minutes, and many men have only occasional episodes.
- **Ongoing tension** typically improves over **several weeks** of daily relaxation and stretching.
- In the Stanford study, meaningful improvement was first seen after a **median of five therapy sessions**.

Progress is rarely a straight line. Flares during stressful weeks are normal and do not mean you are back to square one.

## When to see a doctor

Get checked if you have:

- Pain lasting more than a few weeks or that keeps returning
- **Fever**, burning when you pee, or discharge
- **Blood** in your urine or stool
- Difficulty passing urine
- Unexplained weight loss or changes in bowel habits

Your doctor can rule out infection and other causes before treating it as a muscle issue. For a broader look at pain in this area, see our guide to [pelvic floor pain in men](/blog/pelvic-floor-pain).

## The bottom line

Pelvis muscle spasms happen when your pelvic floor gets stuck in a clench. Stress, sitting, straining and over-training are common triggers, and the pain is often misread as prostatitis. The fix starts with relaxation: stop squeezing, breathe into your belly, stretch, use heat and consider specialist physical therapy. Once the muscle can let go, balanced training helps keep it that way.
