Most men who look into yoga for pelvic floor health are hoping for a gentler alternative to kegels. The better way to see it: yoga fills the gaps kegels leave behind.

Kegels teach the muscle to squeeze. Yoga teaches it to squeeze **and release**, in rhythm with your breath and your hips. A pelvic floor that can do both is the one that gives you firmer erections, better ejaculatory control and a bladder that behaves.

Here is what the research says, which poses are worth your time, and how to combine them with targeted training.

## What yoga actually does to the pelvic floor

Your [pelvic floor](/blog/what-is-the-pelvic-floor) is a sling of muscle running from your pubic bone to your tailbone. It holds up your bladder and bowel, closes off the urethra, and clamps down on the veins at the base of the penis during an erection.

It does not work alone. It is the floor of a pressure system with your diaphragm as the roof and your deep abdominal muscles as the walls. When you inhale, the diaphragm drops and the pelvic floor gently lowers with it. When you exhale, both rise.

Yoga works on that whole system at once:

- **Breath** retrains the diaphragm and pelvic floor to move together instead of bracing
- **Hip-opening poses** release tension in the muscles that attach around the pelvis
- **Strength poses** such as bridge and chair load the pelvic floor alongside the glutes and deep core
- **Mula bandha**, the "root lock" in traditional practice, is essentially a held pelvic floor contraction

That last one matters. Yoga has included a pelvic floor squeeze for centuries — it just never called it a kegel.

## What the research shows

The evidence for yoga is younger and smaller than the evidence for direct pelvic floor training, so it helps to be precise.

**Premature ejaculation.** In a 2007 trial published in the [Journal of Sexual Medicine](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17888067/), Dhikav and colleagues compared a yoga program with the antidepressant fluoxetine in men with PE. All 38 men in the yoga group showed statistically significant improvement, rated good in 25 and fair in 13. In the fluoxetine group, 25 of 30 improved.

A [2025 meta-analysis in the International Journal of Yoga](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41743299/) pooled the available studies and found a significant positive effect of yoga on PE, with no significant side effects reported in the yoga groups. The authors were clear about the limits: only five studies qualified, samples were small, and results varied a lot between them.

**Incontinence.** Most yoga-and-bladder research has been done in women. A 2026 systematic review in Archives of Gerontology and Geriatrics covering 20 trials found pelvic floor muscle training performed similarly to yoga and Pilates for urinary incontinence in older women. There is no reason the mechanics differ much for men, but the direct male data is thin.

**Erections.** There are no strong yoga-specific trials for erectile function. The best evidence here belongs to targeted pelvic floor training: in Dorey's 2004 randomised trial, 40% of men with ED regained normal function after pelvic floor exercises and another 35.5% improved.

The honest summary: **yoga is a useful partner to kegels, not a replacement for them.**

## 7 yoga poses for the pelvic floor

Work through these slowly. Breathe through your nose, and let each exhale be longer than each inhale.

1. **Diaphragmatic breathing (5 minutes).** Lie on your back, knees bent, one hand on your belly. Inhale and feel your belly rise and your pelvic floor soften downward. Exhale and feel it gently lift. This is the foundation for everything else.

2. **Cat-cow (10 slow rounds).** On hands and knees, inhale as you drop your belly and lift your tailbone. Exhale as you round your spine and tuck your tailbone, adding a light pelvic floor lift at the end of the exhale.

3. **Bridge pose (8-10 reps, then a 30-second hold).** Lie on your back, feet flat. Exhale, lightly contract your pelvic floor and lift your hips. Lower on the inhale and let the muscle release fully. This builds strength with your glutes doing the heavy work.

4. **Chair pose (3 holds of 20-30 seconds).** Stand with feet together and sit back as if into a chair. Keep breathing — no breath-holding. The pelvic floor works as a stabiliser here.

5. **Deep squat, or malasana (60 seconds).** Feet wider than hips, heels down if you can, elbows pressing knees apart. Breathe into the pelvis and let it widen and relax. Use a yoga block under your seat if your heels lift.

6. **Happy baby (60 seconds).** On your back, grab the outsides of your feet with knees wide. Rock gently side to side. This is one of the best poses for a pelvic floor that is holding too much tension.

7. **Child pose with breathing (90 seconds).** Knees wide, hips back to heels, forehead down. Breathe into your lower back and pelvis and let everything soften.

That sequence takes about 15 minutes. If you also want dedicated mobility work, our guide to [pelvic floor stretches for men](/blog/pelvic-floor-stretches) goes deeper into the release side.

## Tight vs weak: why the balance matters

Not every pelvic floor problem is a weakness problem. Some men have a pelvic floor that is **overactive** — it stays clenched all day. The [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/14459-pelvic-floor-dysfunction) describes pelvic floor dysfunction as difficulty relaxing and coordinating these muscles, not only weakness.

A tight pelvic floor can show up as pelvic or perineal pain, a weak or hesitant urine stream, pain after ejaculation, or [erections that fade](/blog/how-to-stay-hard-during-sex). Hammering out hundreds of kegels on top of that can make it worse.

This is where yoga earns its place. The relaxing poses — deep squat, happy baby, child pose — and long exhales teach the muscle to let go. If you have ongoing pain, favour those and go easy on long mula bandha holds until a pelvic floor physical therapist has assessed you.

## Combining yoga with kegels

The most effective setup for most men is simple: short daily kegel sets for targeted strength, plus yoga a few times a week for breath, mobility and relaxation.

A realistic week looks like this:

- **Daily:** 2-3 short kegel sessions, mixing quick flicks with 5-10 second holds and full releases
- **3-4 times a week:** the 15-minute yoga sequence above
- **Every session:** finish each contraction with a complete relaxation of the same length

## What progress looks like

Most men notice the breathing and relaxation changes first, often within one to two weeks — less clenching, easier urination, less pelvic tension at the end of the day.

Strength changes take longer. Pelvic floor training studies such as Dorey's ran for three to six months, and that is the timescale to judge real change in erections, leaking or ejaculatory control. The PE yoga trials also ran for weeks, not days.

A useful check at around eight weeks: can you hold a clean 10-second contraction, release it completely, and repeat that ten times without your glutes or breath taking over? If yes, the training is working.

## Common questions

**Do I need to be flexible?** No. Use a block under your seat in the squat and bend your knees as much as you need. The pelvic floor benefits come from breath and gentle loading, not from deep range.

**Hot yoga or regular?** It makes no difference to the pelvic floor. Pick the class you will actually keep going to.

**Should I contract during every pose?** No. Contract lightly on exhales in strength poses like bridge and chair. In the hip-opening poses, the goal is the opposite — let it relax.

## The takeaway

Yoga will not replace targeted pelvic floor training, but it makes that training work better. It teaches the muscle to relax as well as it contracts, it opens up the hips around it, and it gives you a breathing pattern that supports every rep. Do kegels for strength, do yoga for balance, and give it two to three months before you judge the results.
