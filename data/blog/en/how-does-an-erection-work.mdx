How does an erection work? Most men would answer "blood flows in," which is true and leaves out the interesting part. Blood is only half the job. The other half is keeping it there, and that depends on nerves, smooth muscle, a tough fibrous sheath and the pelvic floor all working in sequence.

Knowing the sequence is useful. When an erection falters, the failure sits at a specific link in the chain, and some of those links respond to training.

## The Parts Involved

An erection runs on three chambers of spongy tissue. Two sit side by side along the top of the penis and do most of the work. These are the **corpora cavernosa**. The third, the **corpus spongiosum**, runs underneath, surrounds the urethra and widens into the glans.

The corpora cavernosa are wrapped in the **tunica albuginea**, a tough sheath that behaves like the wall of a fire hose. Inside the chambers, the tissue is packed with blood spaces called sinusoids, lined with smooth muscle.

Here is the fact that surprises most men. According to a [widely cited review by Dean and Lue](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1351051/) from UCSF, that smooth muscle stays **tonically contracted when you are soft**, letting in only enough blood to feed the tissue. A [flaccid penis](/blog/flaccid-penis) is a penis actively held in a low-flow state. Getting hard means letting go.

## How an Erection Works, Step by Step

### 1. The Signal Starts

Erections come in three types, per Dean and Lue. Psychogenic erections start with thoughts or visual stimuli. Reflexogenic erections start with touch, and they are preserved in many men with upper spinal cord injury. Nocturnal erections happen mostly during REM sleep.

Whatever the trigger, the message arrives through the cavernous nerves, which release neurotransmitters at the penis.

### 2. The Smooth Muscle Relaxes

Those neurotransmitters start the nitric oxide pathway. Nitric oxide raises a messenger called cGMP, and cGMP lowers calcium inside the smooth muscle cells, so the muscle lets go.

An enzyme called PDE5 breaks cGMP down. This is exactly where [PDE5 inhibitors](/blog/pde5-inhibitors-for-erectile-dysfunction) such as sildenafil act: they block that enzyme so the signal lasts longer. It also explains why those pills still need arousal to work. They amplify a signal rather than create one.

### 3. Blood Rushes In

With the muscle relaxed, the arteries and arterioles dilate and blood flow jumps. The sinusoids expand and fill. This is the visible part, as the penis lengthens and swells.

### 4. The Exit Gets Squeezed Shut

This is the step most men have never heard of. As the sinusoids expand, they press the small veins beneath the tunica against that tough outer sheath. Then the tunica stretches to its capacity and closes the emissary veins that pass through it.

Dean and Lue describe the result as a sharp reduction in venous outflow. Blood is still coming in, but very little gets out, so pressure climbs. Doctors call this **veno-occlusion**. It is the reason an erection can hold without constant high flow.

### 5. The Pelvic Floor Adds the Final Squeeze

Pressure from blood alone gets you to the full-erection phase. A further rise, to several hundred millimeters of mercury, comes when the **ischiocavernosus muscles** contract. Dean and Lue call this the rigid-erection phase.

A small [1986 study in The Journal of Urology](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3761465/) by Lavoisier and colleagues showed this directly. In nine men with erectile dysfunction, given an artificial erection for the test, voluntary contractions of the ischiocavernosus raised pressure inside the penis by 100 to 525 mm Hg, in step with the muscle's electrical activity.

The bulbocavernosus muscle works alongside it, compressing the spongiosum and penile veins. It also provides the rhythmic contractions needed for ejaculation.

### 6. Everything Reverses

After ejaculation or when arousal fades, the smooth muscle contracts again, inflow drops, the veins reopen and blood drains out. Dean and Lue note that the penis stays in a moderate state of contraction at rest, which is why it shrinks further in cold weather.

## Why Erections Fail

Each step is a possible weak point. Dean and Lue classify causes of erectile problems along these lines:

- **Signal problems:** stress, anxiety or nerve damage from injury, surgery or diabetes
- **Hormone problems:** [low testosterone](/blog/low-testosterone-symptoms-in-men) can reduce desire and erectile function
- **Inflow problems:** narrowed arteries reduce the blood reaching the penis
- **Veno-occlusive problems:** Peyronie's disease, age or diabetes can stop the tunica from compressing veins, so blood leaks out and erections fade

Occasional misses are normal. The [NHS says](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/erection-problems-erectile-dysfunction/) most men sometimes fail to get or keep an erection, usually because of stress, tiredness or too much alcohol. If it keeps happening, see a GP. If you want a first read on your own pattern, our guide on [how to check for ED](/blog/how-to-check-for-ed) walks through it.

## The Part You Can Train

You cannot directly flex your smooth muscle or your veins. You can train the muscles behind step five. The ischiocavernosus and bulbocavernosus are skeletal muscles under voluntary control, and they sit in the same group you work during a kegel.

The best-known evidence is a [randomized trial by Dorey and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15527607/). After six months of pelvic floor exercises with biofeedback and lifestyle advice, about 40 percent of men with erectile dysfunction regained normal function and another 35 percent improved. That does not mean kegels help every cause. Training cannot open a blocked artery, but it can strengthen one real link in the chain.

A simple way to start:

1. **Find the muscle.** Tighten as if stopping urine flow and lifting your testicles slightly. Keep your buttocks, thighs and abs relaxed.
2. **Hold for 3 to 5 seconds,** then release fully for the same time.
3. **Repeat 10 times,** two or three sessions a day.
4. **Add quick pulses.** Finish each set with 5 to 10 fast squeezes, since the rigid phase relies on strong, brief contractions.

## What Progress Looks Like

Do not expect changes overnight. The Dorey trial ran for six months, so think in months, not days. Many men notice better muscle control first, such as longer holds and an easier time isolating the right muscles. Any change in firmness tends to come later and varies from man to man.

The rest of the chain matters too. The lifestyle basics the NHS recommends support the blood flow and nerve signaling that earlier steps depend on: eat a healthy diet, exercise daily, keep alcohol within limits and reduce stress. ## The Bottom Line

An erection is a relay. Nerves signal, smooth muscle relaxes, blood flows in, veins get pinned shut, and the pelvic floor squeezes pressure to its peak. Most of that chain runs on autopilot, but the last link is a muscle you can train.
